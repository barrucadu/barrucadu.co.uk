<h2 style="display: none" markdown="1">Introduction</h2>

That's right, I have entered the exciting world of homeownership!  This isn't a
step I took entirely willingly (my landlord decided to sell the flat I was
renting, and evicted me), but it's one I've been considering for some years now
as renting sucks.

Buying a house is a weirdly complicated process, so this post attempts to
demystify it a bit by going into the concrete details and costs relevant to *my*
purchase.  Your House May Vary.

## What did it cost?

Let's start with the headline figures and break that down in the subsequent
sections.

For a **£350,000** 4-bedroom house I spent:

- **Deposit:** £125,000
- **Mortgage Fees:** £1099
- **Surveys:** £1441
- **Building Insurance:** £434.38
- **Legal Fees (inc. postage of documentation):** £2207.95
- **Stamp Duty:** £2500
- **Moving:** £1804
- **Essential Furnishings:** £3538.24

**Total:**  £138,024.57

"Essential Furnishings" covers: a new bed (but I got an expensive mattress, so
you could shave a thousand pounds off that), bedroom curtains, and a washing
machine (the sellers left all the other appliances).


## Choosing the house

I needed somewhere within commuting distance of London, as I have to come into
the office once a week.  But I can put up with quite a bit of inconvenience one
day a week, so I was looking at places up to 2 hours away.

I spent several hours on [Rightmove][], looking for houses I liked and then
comparing them.

I scored candidates red, amber, and green on three axes:

- **Internet speed:** symmetric gigabit or faster is green; 500Mbps or faster download is amber
- **Shopping:** a supermarket within a 20m walk is green; 45m is amber
- **Commute:** 1h30m or less is green; 2h or less is amber

I also restricted my search to detached or semi-detached freehold houses,
because fuck leasehold&mdash;that's just renting by another name.

I then looked at the difference in costs, specifically:

- Mortgage payments there vs rent payments here
- Council tax there vs council tax here
- Commute there vs commute here

I assumed bills and other spending would remain largely the same.  Of note is
that I'd been putting £750/month aside towards a house deposit, so I had that
bit of wiggle room too.

I quickly settled on Peterborough as being a good location.  It's far enough
away from London that house prices are relatively low, but it's a major
transport hub so the commute isn't too bad.  When I've looked into houses in
previous years, Peterborough came out on top then too.

I went to view my top candidate a few days later, a Friday, and put an offer in
on the Monday.

<konata:glasses>
Now that I've run the numbers again, and in more detail, with an actual house
chosen, there are a few extra things I should have considered:

- It's recommended to put aside 1% of the house value each year for maintenance
- Your mortgage provider will require you to have building insurance
- You will probably also want to overpay the mortgage

That comes to an additional £300 + £40 + £500/month for me.  On the positive
side, it looks like my utility bills will be around £100/month lower due to
picking different suppliers.
</konata>

## How do you buy a house?

I relied on these two guides from Which? to come up with my own checklist:

- [How to buy a house](https://www.which.co.uk/money/mortgages-and-property/first-time-buyers/buying-a-home/how-to-buy-a-house-a8zHm0a1JZsP)
- [Moving house checklist](https://www.which.co.uk/money/mortgages-and-property/home-movers/moving-house/moving-house-checklist-aMppD6l8mlbQ)

I learned some things along the way, too.  Here's my recommendation:

1. **Get a decision in principle (DIP) for a mortgage.**  Buying a house is a
   game of nobody wanting to commit to anything until the very last moment.  A
   DIP is a document from a bank which essentially says "if we were to lend you
   money, which we're not saying we will, it might be in this ballpark".  I used
   [Nationwide][], as they're my primary bank.

2. **Make an offer on a house, and have it accepted.**  In my case, they were
   asking for a range, and I offered the bottom end of that range, which they
   accepted.  **Make sure to specify that this offer is subject to a survey and
   conditional on the property being taken off the market.**  Nothing legally
   binding has happened yet (and won't for months), so if the property remains
   on the market and the seller gets a better offer, they might just drop you.

3. **Enlist a conveyancer.**  You'll want one [registered with the CLC][].  The
   estate agent may have one they recommend, but it's a bad idea to go with
   their recommendation without doing some research first (as the agent is
   likely to recommend one who'll pay them a commission, rather than one who is
   good).  However, in my case, the agent recommended [Larrington & Co][], who
   had glowing reviews that specifically mentioned how quick the process was,
   and I wanted quick (I had an eviction looming!), so I went with them.

4. **Apply for a mortgage.**  Ideally with the bank you have your DIP with, as
   they're likely to accept you.  In my case, I was instantly rejected by my
   bank ([Nationwide][]) with no reason given, so I scrambled and applied for a
   mortgage for almost the same interest rate with a different bank
   ([Halifax][]).

5. **Book a building survey.**  The seller is under no obligation to tell you
   about (most) problems.  So you'll want a building survey by [a surveyor
   registered with RICS][] to check it out.  Most people get a "level 2" survey,
   but I went for a "level 3" survey, which is the most comprehensive type.  I
   used [Crest Surveyors][].

6. **Book electric / gas / plumbing surveys.**  The building survey is just a
   visual inspection, nothing else.  The survey report will strongly recommend
   you get an electrician and a plumber to check it out too.  I used [Home
   Service Reports][] and got their combined "[Home Service Report & Electrical
   Installation Condition Report][]", though it seems they outsourced the work
   to the [Gas-Elec Group][].

7. **Wait until everyone's ready to exchange contracts.**  This part takes a
   long time, and there's little to do beyond reply to questions from your
   conveyancer.  You'll likely need to:

    - Provide documentation to the conveyancer and mortgage provider (proof of
      identity and also proof of funds for the deposit will definitely be
      needed).

    - Sign legal things.

    - Get quotes from the estate agent for any major works identified by the
      surveys (if something is both expensive and required, consider lowering
      your offer or requiring the sellers to get it done before completion).
      Thankfully nothing major came up in my case.

    - Arrange buildings insurance (I used [Halifax][]).

    - Nudge the conveyancer if things seem to be moving slowly.

8. **Exchange contracts.**  This is the point at which the purchase becomes
   legally binding, and you have a concrete date that the property becomes
   yours, likely a week or two later.  Though, the exact time of day you get it
   will be whenever, on that day, all the bank transfers resolve.

9. **Organise the move.**  Once you have the completion date set in stone, you
   can:
 
    - Book removals.  I used [3 Rivers Waste Clearance][] to dispose of the
      things I wasn't moving, and then [In-Excess Removals][] to move the rest.

    - [Inform your local council][] that you're moving (and also your new
      council if you're moving to a new area).

    - Terminate your current utility accounts and set up new ones.  You'll
      probably need to provide meter readings on the actual move date, too..

    - Update your address with everyone who cares (TTRPG kickstarters, banks,
      work, &hellip;).

    - Arrange [post redirection][] with Royal Mail.

    - Update the [Electoral Roll][].

The average time to purchase a house is around 12 weeks, so there's a lot of
waiting involved.  I had my offer accepted on the 10th of March, and we
exchanged contracts on the 22nd of May (51 business days) to complete on the
29th (56 business days).

## Moving

I completed on a Thursday, so I took that Thursday and Friday off work, and the
whole of the following week too.  Since I'd extended my tenancy by two weeks I
didn't actually need to move on completion day, so I took the time to do the
process gradually.  I also had to juggle moving with being on call for work, due
to an unfortunate timing clash I wasn't able to totally resolve.

I definitely recommend taking multiple days to move.

I visited the house three times before the actual move date, and each time I
took a few awkward bulky items with me (e.g. a chess set, which absolutely
wasn't going to fit in any of my boxes) to make the rest of the packing easier.

- On the **Thursday** I collected the keys, measured all the bedrooms up for
  furniture, did some shopping for toiletries and cleaning supplies, and rang a
  painter to arrange getting a quote to redo a few rooms.

- On the **Monday** I returned to show the painter the house, did a bit more
  shopping, and took some measurements I'd forgotten about last time.

- On the **Wednesday** I visited a carpet showroom, arranged to get a quote for
  that, and finished off the shopping.

That might sound like a lot of shopping, but I'd arranged things so I would
completely run out of *everything* by the move date: so I needed to buy
toiletries, cleaning supplies, cooking oils & sauces, herbs, spices, etc.
That's quite a lot of stuff when you're outfitting a house!

The move was on the Thursday (a week after completion), after which I took a
couple of days to unpack and relax (and receive deliveries I'd arranged).  I
then returned to the flat on the Sunday to give it a really thorough clean, take
final electricity & gas meter readings, and drop off the keys.


## Furnishing & decoration

Long ago, I vowed that when I was free of landlords I would have a colourful and
interesting home.

In the weeks and months before the move, I spent a lot of time thinking about
how I wanted to arrange the house.  What the colour theme of each room would be,
what furniture will be needed where, how to make adjacent rooms feel like
cohesive parts of a larger whole, and so on.  From this I amassed a big list of
furniture, decorations, and other items to potentially buy.  Even down to the
mundane things like bathroom bins.

While I've not had the house redecorated yet, here's the plan:

- **Master bedroom:**
    - [Green walls][wm], neutral bedsheets, [patterned curtains][cm], cream carpet
    - Art prints by [Ulla Thynell][]
- **Guest bedroom:**
    - [Yellow walls][wg], neutral bedsheets, [patterned curtains][cg] (not exactly this one, but the one I picked is similar and no longer available), cream carpet
    - Art prints by [Pauhami][]
- **Office:**
    - [Charcoal walls][wf], roman blinds TBD, cream carpet
    - My current art prints from the [H. P. Lovecraft Historical Society][]
- **Living room:**
    - [Charcoal walls][wf], curtains TBD, cream carpet
    - Art prints by [Goran Gligović][]
- **Entrance hall / stairs / upper landing:**
    - Cream carpet (stairs & upper landing) / wood floor (entrance hall) (already in place)
    - Art prints by [Ze Burnay][]
- **Conservatory:**
    - Tile floor (already in place)
    - Art prints by [M'fanwy Dean][], or maybe [H. B. Tyson][]

I've tried to create a few themes: the bedrooms are fairytale themed; both "fun"
rooms (office & living room) have very similar decor; the Ze Burnay prints are
not only physically between the more fairytale-like prints of Ulla Thynell and
Pauhami and the weirder prints of Goran Gligovic and the HPLHS, their weird
nature theme kind of bridges the thematic gap too; and the consistent carpeting
acts as a unifying item.

I haven't firmly settled on those specific paints yet, but it'll be something
like those even if it's not those exact shades.  I'm also carefully picking out
the exact prints I want.  It looks like it'll cost around £6,500 for both the
painting and carpeting (covering materials, labour, stripping the wallpaper from
the guest bedroom, and removing & disposing of the current carpet).

Hopefully everything will be done in another month or two, and I'll have a
colourful comfy home to live in, work from, and host guests.
